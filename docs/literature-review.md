## 1. Introduction

The proliferation of digital communication channels—encompassing short messaging services (SMS), Telegram, and electronic mail—has catalyzed a catastrophic rise in social engineering and financial fraud. Deceptive messaging campaigns, notably phishing, smishing, and targeted impersonation attacks, represent one of the primary attack vectors compromising consumer privacy, organizational networks, and digital financial ecosystems. The Anti-Phishing Working Group observed about 3.8 million phishing attacks in 2025 and reports a growing use of SMS for phishing (APWG, 2026). Modern threat actors leverage sophisticated deception strategies, including psychological urgency, authority pretexting, dynamic link redirection, and homoglyphic lexical obfuscation. In response, modern cybersecurity cannot rely solely on static heuristic filters, legacy regular expressions, or reactive domain blacklists.

To counteract these escalating threats, modern defense mechanisms increasingly employ Artificial Intelligence (AI), Machine Learning (ML), and Natural Language Processing (NLP). However, despite considerable research into standalone classification algorithms, a pronounced structural disconnect persists between theoretical model architectures and practical, deployable security systems. Much of the academic literature focuses exclusively on maximizing accuracy metrics on historical, static benchmarks without addressing production constraints: inference latency, model interpretability, adversarial evasion resistance, scalable client-server architectures, and proactive user prevention mechanics. 

This literature review provides a comprehensive, critical evaluation of the state of the art in automated fraudulent message detection and secure web platform engineering. Its purpose is to lay the empirical and architectural foundation for the diploma project: **"Intelligent Web Platform for Analysis, Detection, and Prevention of Fraudulent Messages Using Machine Learning Methods."** The review covers nineteen sources: sixteen peer-reviewed papers, two books and one survey preprint, published by IEEE, ACM, Elsevier, Springer, MDPI, USENIX and others. Eleven of them were published in 2021–2025. The analysis covers seven thematic areas:
1. Phishing and fraud detection using NLP and ML;
2. Text classification representations (TF-IDF, dense embeddings, transformers);
3. Comparative model performance across traditional classifiers, recurrent neural networks, and transformer-based architectures;
4. Datasets and evaluation protocols for spam and phishing;
5. Web platform architectures and engineering patterns for real-time security analysis;
6. Social engineering taxonomies and human deception mechanisms.
7. Recent work (2023–2025) on SMS and smishing detection.

The review concludes with a structured analysis table and a rigorous synthesis of identified research gaps, detailing how the proposed web platform addresses unresolved challenges in the field.

## 2. Systematic Thematic Review of Literature

### 2.1. Phishing and Fraud Detection Using NLP and Machine Learning

#### Source 1: Alhogail, A., & Alsabih, A. (2021). Applying machine learning and natural language processing to detect phishing email. *Computers & Security*, 110, 102414.

Alhogail and Alsabih investigate the challenge of detecting phishing communications by analyzing the semantic and psychological structures embedded within message bodies. Recognizing that malicious actors exploit human cognitive biases—such as fear, urgency, and greed—the authors propose a deep learning framework founded on Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN) integrated with advanced NLP preprocessing. Traditional text classification models frequently treat documents as flat bags of words or linear sequences, often missing non-consecutive semantic associations. By structuring message text as word co-occurrence and document graphs, the authors' GCN framework captures high-order semantic dependencies and relational context across disparate sentences, achieving a remarkable detection accuracy of 98.2% and a low false-positive rate of 0.015 across standardized benchmarks.

The methodology relies on extensive text normalization, tokenization, stop-word removal, and syntactic parsing, followed by graph construction where nodes represent distinct words and documents, and edges denote point-wise mutual information (PMI) and term frequencies. The resulting graph representations are propagated through multiple graph convolutional layers to learn latent embeddings that differentiate legitimate administrative notices from deceptive social engineering lures. The authors establish that semantic relationships provide substantially stronger resistance against basic keyword obfuscation than conventional n-gram features.

However, a critical limitation of Alhogail and Alsabih's approach lies in the computational complexity of graph construction and inference. Building dynamic graph topologies for real-time incoming messages introduces notable computational latency, making client-side or high-throughput API deployment problematic. Furthermore, the model was trained and evaluated strictly on long-form email corpora; its capability to maintain high fidelity on short, highly abbreviated, and noisy texts (such as SMS or chat messages) remains unproven. For the current diploma project, this study underscores the immense value of semantic feature relationships while demonstrating the necessity of adopting lower-latency embedding architectures (e.g., fine-tuned lightweight transformers) that can operate efficiently within an interactive web platform.

#### Source 2: Sahoo, D., Liu, C., & Hoi, S. C. H. (2017). Malicious URL Detection using Machine Learning: A Survey. arXiv preprint arXiv:1701.07179.

Because fraudulent messages almost ubiquitously contain hyperlinks directing victims to credential-harvesting landing pages or malware drops, Sahoo, Liu, and Hoi provide a comprehensive, foundational survey on malicious URL detection using machine learning. The authors formulate a multi-dimensional taxonomy of URL features, categorizing them into lexical features (character lengths, token counts, entropy, presence of IP addresses, top-level domain distributions), host-based features (WHOIS registration metadata, DNS records, geographic location), content-based features (HTML structures, JavaScript execution patterns), and contextual reputation indicators. They evaluate batch learning classifiers against online machine learning paradigms that adapt dynamically to evolving threat distributions.

The survey demonstrates that while content-based and host-based features offer high detection fidelity, they incur severe latency penalties due to network querying, DNS lookups, and active web scraping. In contrast, lexical URL analysis enables rapid, lightweight static screening capable of blocking malicious links in real time. Sahoo et al. synthesize mathematical formulations for online gradient descent and passive-aggressive algorithms, emphasizing that malicious URL distributions undergo rapid concept drift as attackers generate ephemeral domains via domain generation algorithms (DGAs) and URL shorteners.

The primary limitation of Sahoo et al.'s survey in the context of messaging fraud is its isolation of URLs from their carrier message text. Threat actors frequently exploit reputable, benign hosting providers (e.g., Google Forms, Canva, Notion, Firebase) to craft phishing landing pages, rendering pure lexical URL classifiers blind to the underlying fraud. For our diploma project, Sahoo et al.'s findings establish the imperative of designing a hybrid, multi-stage detection engine: lexical URL inspection must act as an auxiliary filter that complements, rather than replaces, deep semantic text analysis of the message body itself.

### 2.2. Text Classification Representations (TF-IDF, Embeddings, and Transformers)

#### Source 3: Devlin, J., Chang, M.-W., Lee, K., & Toutanova, K. (2019). BERT: Pre-training of Deep Bidirectional Transformers for Language Understanding. In *Proceedings of the 2019 Conference of the North American Chapter of the Association for Computational Linguistics: Human Language Technologies (NAACL-HLT 2019)*, pages 4171–4186.

In this foundational publication, Devlin and colleagues introduced BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers), revolutionizing natural language processing by overcoming the strict unidirectional constraints of previous language representation models. Unlike traditional static word embeddings (Word2Vec, GloVe) which assign a single fixed vector to each token regardless of context, and unlike unidirectional recurrent models, BERT utilizes a multi-layer bidirectional Transformer encoder based on self-attention mechanisms. Pre-trained on vast unlabeled corpora using Masked Language Modeling (MLM) and Next Sentence Prediction (NSP), BERT captures deep syntactic, semantic, and contextual nuances across bidirectional word sequences.

The application of BERT to fraudulent text detection fundamentally alters feature representation. Fraudulent messages frequently use polysemous words, subtle conversational framing, and contextual deception cues where the meaning of a word depends entirely on the surrounding sentence structure (e.g., distinguishing between an authentic banking notice "Your account balance is updated" and a coercive phishing scam "Your account will be suspended unless updated immediately"). Devlin et al. showed that fine-tuning pre-trained BERT architectures on domain-specific downstream classification tasks yields state-of-the-art results across diverse NLP benchmarks with minimal task-specific architectural alterations.

Despite its exceptional semantic representation capabilities, BERT presents substantial operational overhead. BERT-Base encompasses 110 million parameters, requiring considerable computational memory and graphical processing units (GPUs) for both fine-tuning and inference. Deploying vanilla BERT within a real-time web platform poses scalability and response latency challenges under concurrent user loads. Consequently, this foundational work motivates our diploma project to leverage transformer representations while critically exploring distilled or optimized variants (such as DistilBERT, RoBERTa, or quantized ONNX runtimes) to preserve sub-second response times on standard web application servers.

#### Source 4: Salloum, S. A., Gaber, T., Vadera, S., & Shaalan, K. (2021). Phishing Email Detection Using Natural Language Processing Techniques: A Literature Survey. *Procedia Computer Science*, 189, 19–28.

Salloum et al. present a systematic literature review categorizing NLP feature extraction methodologies and machine learning models applied specifically to phishing detection. The authors analyze the progression of linguistic feature engineering from early morphological analysis, bag-of-words, and TF-IDF (Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency) to semantic embeddings and sentiment-oriented feature extractors. They trace how variations in feature dimensionality and representation depth impact the downstream classification performance of Support Vector Machines, Naive Bayes, Decision Trees, and Multi-Layer Perceptrons.

The authors highlight that traditional frequency-based text representations (such as TF-IDF) perform adequately on rigid, stereotypical spam containing repetitive commercial keywords. However, they demonstrate that TF-IDF degrades severely when applied to targeted spear-phishing and modern conversational fraud. Because TF-IDF relies entirely on exact lexical matches and statistical word distributions, attackers easily bypass TF-IDF filters through deliberate misspellings, synonym substitution, zero-width characters, and non-standard syntax. Salloum et al. emphasize that incorporating NLP semantic features—such as modal verb analysis, imperative sentence indicators, and urgent sentiment polarity—significantly elevates classifier resilience.

A notable deficiency in Salloum et al.'s review is the lack of empirical performance comparisons under identical environmental benchmarks and the absence of system engineering discussions. The authors review models in abstract theoretical silos, omitting critical deployment parameters such as preprocessing overhead, API throughput, and false-positive implications on user productivity. In our diploma work, Salloum et al.'s analysis validates the decision to transition beyond basic TF-IDF representations toward contextual semantic vectors, while demonstrating the utility of hybrid feature extraction where structural linguistic markers complement statistical classifiers.

### 2.3. Comparative Analysis of Machine Learning Models (SVM, Random Forest, LSTM, BERT)

#### Source 5: Ghourabi, A., Mahmood, M. A., & Alzubi, Q. M. (2020). A Hybrid CNN-LSTM Model for SMS Spam Detection in Arabic and English Messages. *Future Internet*, 12(9), 156.

Ghourabi, Mahmood, and Alzubi investigate the efficacy of deep sequential neural network architectures for detecting spam and malicious SMS messages across English and Arabic languages. The authors address the structural limitations of standalone convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and recurrent neural networks (RNNs) by designing a hybrid CNN-LSTM pipeline. In this architecture, a convolutional layer extracts localized, position-invariant n-gram feature maps from word embedding vectors, which are subsequently fed into Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) units to capture long-range temporal dependencies and word ordering throughout the text sequence.

The authors conduct rigorous empirical comparisons pitting their hybrid model against traditional machine learning classifiers, including Support Vector Machines (SVM), Random Forest (RF), Naive Bayes (NB), and standalone CNN and LSTM networks. On the standard English SMS Spam Collection dataset, the hybrid CNN-LSTM achieved an exceptional classification accuracy of 98.37%, with an F1-score of 0.935, outperforming standalone SVM (97.64%) and Random Forest (97.90%). The authors demonstrate that the sequential memory cells of LSTMs resolve vanishing gradient challenges, allowing the model to detect fraudulent contexts even when manipulative phrases are separated by neutral filler words.

Nevertheless, Ghourabi et al.'s study reveals key practical drawbacks. The hybrid CNN-LSTM model requires extensive training durations and displays susceptibility to overfitting when trained on small, imbalanced datasets. Furthermore, like most deep recurrent networks, the architecture functions as an opaque black box, offering zero explanation regarding which specific tokens triggered the spam classification. In an intelligent web platform dedicated not only to detection but also to user education and attack prevention, black-box classification is insufficient. Our diploma project takes Ghourabi et al.'s comparative benchmark as an empirical baseline, extending it by integrating Explainable AI (XAI) layers and comparing sequential neural models with fine-tuned transformers.

#### Source 6: Seo, J. W., Lee, J. S., Kim, H., Lee, J., Han, S., Cho, J., & Lee, C.-H. (2024). On-Device Smishing Classifier Resistant to Text Evasion Attack. *IEEE Access*, 12, 4762–4779.

In a highly relevant industrial research paper from Samsung Research, Seo and colleagues confront the vulnerability of deep learning smishing classifiers to adversarial text evasion attacks. Attackers routinely employ intentional typographic permutations—including leetspeak (e.g., replacing 'E' with '3'), homoglyphic character replacements (substituting Latin characters with visually identical Cyrillic glyphs), zero-width whitespace insertions, and delimiter padding—to circumvent standard tokenizers and machine learning models. To resolve this, the authors introduce an on-device deep learning smishing classification architecture specifically hardened against adversarial evasion.

Seo et al. construct a massive, realistic dataset comprising approximately 250,000 real-world smishing messages and 950,000 benign messages. They develop an adversarial evaluation tool termed EVA (Evasion Variant Analyzer) that automatically generates realistic typographic mutations. The authors propose a lightweight, robust neural architecture utilizing character-level and sub-word tokenization coupled with adversarial data augmentation during training. The resulting model consumes a mere 127 kB of memory footprint, operates locally without leaking sensitive user communications to cloud infrastructure, and achieves over 98.5% detection accuracy while maintaining high robustness against character manipulation attacks.

While Seo et al. excel in adversarial hardening and on-device optimization, their paradigm intentionally restricts the system from querying external threat intelligence platforms, domain reputation databases, or computationally rich backend language models. Consequently, the model cannot verify whether an un-obfuscated URL redirects to a newly registered phishing domain or inspect live web content. For our diploma project, Seo et al.'s work provides critical guidance for building resilient text normalization preprocessors that strip homoglyphs and leetspeak, ensuring our web platform's server-side ML model is not easily blinded by basic adversarial evasion tactics.

#### Source 7: Mishra, S., & Soni, D. (2023). DSmishSMS—A System to Detect Smishing SMS. *Neural Computing and Applications*, 35(7), 4975–4992.

Mishra and Soni address the unique linguistic constraints of smishing (SMS phishing) messages—characterized by short sequence lengths, informal slang, heavy contractions, and embedded deceptive links. The authors propose *DSmishSMS*, an automated detection system featuring a coordinated two-phase analysis pipeline. The first phase executes a domain-checking protocol that parses URLs extracted from the message, interrogates domain WHOIS age, evaluates IP address redirections, and cross-references blacklist repositories. If the link is ambiguous, the second phase activates a machine learning classification engine that processes extracted lexical and syntactic text features using artificial neural networks (ANN) and backpropagation algorithms.

The authors evaluate *DSmishSMS* against an ensemble of baseline machine learning algorithms, including Random Forest, SVM with radial basis function (RBF) kernels, Decision Trees, and Naive Bayes, utilizing an expanded SMS dataset containing contemporary COVID-19 relief lures, banking alerts, and lottery scams. Their experimental results demonstrate an overall classification accuracy of 97.93%, with Random Forest and the proposed ANN exhibiting superior resilience to noisy text over simple linear models. The study confirms that combining URL verification with text feature extraction drastically reduces false negatives compared to analyzing text or URLs in isolation.

The primary limitation of *DSmishSMS* is its synchronous architectural coupling and lack of modern transformer integration. If the external domain lookup encounters network timeouts or rate-limiting, the entire detection pipeline stalls, creating unacceptable user latency. Additionally, their text classification phase relies on manually engineered features rather than deep contextual embeddings. In our diploma project, we build upon Mishra and Soni's two-stage concept by implementing an asynchronous, decoupled microservices architecture where URL reputation checks and transformer-based text analysis execute concurrently, maximizing throughput while eliminating single-point bottlenecks.

### 2.4. Datasets and Benchmarks for Spam and Phishing Detection

#### Source 8: Almeida, T. A., Gómez Hidalgo, J. M., & Yamakami, A. (2011). Contributions to the study of SMS spam filtering: new collection and results. In *Proceedings of the 11th ACM Symposium on Document Engineering (DocEng '11)*, pages 259–262.

In this foundational work, Almeida, Gómez Hidalgo, and Yamakami created the renowned SMS Spam Collection, which has served for over a decade as the benchmark standard for academic research into mobile message classification. The authors addressed a critical deficiency in the research community: the absence of a standardized, public, non-synthetic SMS corpus. They manually gathered, sanitized, and labeled 5,574 legitimate (ham) and malicious (spam/phishing) English SMS messages from diverse real-world sources, including the Grumbletext UK forum, the NUS SMS Corpus, and mobile phone user donations.

Almeida et al. established rigorous baseline evaluation methodologies, comparing various feature extractors (character n-grams, word forms) alongside traditional probabilistic and linear classifiers, including Multinomial Naive Bayes, SVMs, and k-Nearest Neighbors. Their benchmark experiments demonstrated that linear SVMs achieved top-tier performance on the dataset, exhibiting an accuracy of over 97.6% and establishing that statistical text filtering could be viable on resource-constrained mobile telecommunications data.

Despite its foundational importance, the SMS Spam Collection exhibits severe limitations in contemporary cybersecurity contexts. Comprising 4,827 ham messages and only 747 spam messages, the dataset suffers from class imbalance. More critically, collected in 2011, its spam samples represent obsolete commercial marketing spam and primitive advance-fee fraud. Modern deceptive tactics—such as cryptocurrency recovery schemes, two-factor authentication (2FA) bypass lures, multi-stage Telegram redirect chains, and conversational social engineering—are entirely absent. Relying solely on the Almeida corpus produces models that fail to generalize against modern attacks. Our diploma project utilizes this foundational dataset for historical baseline replication while systematically augmenting it with contemporary datasets (e.g., modern Kaggle/HuggingFace smishing corpora) to ensure real-world threat relevance.

#### Source 9: Sahingoz, O. K., Buber, E., Demir, O., & Diri, B. (2019). Machine learning based phishing detection from URLs. *Expert Systems with Applications*, 117, 345–357.

Sahingoz and colleagues tackle the challenge of real-time phishing detection by constructing a large-scale, reproducible URL evaluation framework. The authors curate an extensive empirical dataset consisting of 73,575 URLs (36,400 legitimate URLs extracted from DMOZ directories and 37,175 confirmed phishing URLs harvested from PhishTank). The core contribution of this work is the systematic extraction and comparative evaluation of NLP-based word vectors derived directly from URL strings, parsing domain labels, subdomains, path hierarchies, and query parameters without requiring access to webpage content.

The authors evaluate seven distinct machine learning algorithms: Decision Trees, Random Forest, AdaBoost, K-Nearest Neighbors, Naive Bayes, Support Vector Machines, and Multi-Layer Perceptrons. Furthermore, they implement and benchmark multiple feature representation schemas, including word-level TF-IDF and n-gram vectors. Their findings indicate that ensemble decision tree methods—specifically Random Forest—achieved the highest classification accuracy (97.98%) when paired with word-level NLP representations, outperforming complex individual classifiers while maintaining low computational latency.

However, the methodology of Sahingoz et al. contains critical limitations. By focusing exclusively on URL lexical tokens, the classifier remains completely agnostic to the social engineering text surrounding the URL within a fraudulent message. Furthermore, attackers increasingly obfuscate URLs using multi-hop URL shorteners (e.g., bit.ly, tinyurl) or host malicious redirection pages on legitimate cloud platforms (e.g., Microsoft SharePoint, Google Drive, AWS S3), where the lexical structure of the domain itself appears entirely benign. In our diploma platform, Sahingoz et al.'s findings inform the construction of the URL feature extraction pipeline, but this pipeline is firmly integrated as a secondary feature stream within a holistic NLP framework that analyzes the complete conversational context.

### 2.5. Web Platform Architectures and Engineering for Security Analysis

#### Source 10: Al-Subaiey, A., Al-Thani, M., Alam, N. A., Antora, K. F., Khandakar, A., & Zaman, S. M. A. U. (2024). Novel interpretable and robust web-based AI platform for phishing email detection. *Computers & Electrical Engineering*, 120, 109625.

Al-Subaiey and co-authors present an exceptional, recent advancement bridging the divide between theoretical machine learning research and usable security engineering. Recognizing that conventional phishing detection systems operate as inaccessible, opaque command-line scripts or closed proprietary appliances, the authors design, train, and deploy an end-to-end web-based artificial intelligence platform. The platform couples high-performing machine learning classifiers (trained on comprehensive public datasets achieving an F1-score of 0.99) with a full-stack, responsive web application and Explainable AI (XAI) capabilities.

The architectural core of Al-Subaiey et al.'s platform incorporates SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) and LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations) directly into the web interface. When an analyst or end-user submits a suspicious message, the backend processes the text, computes model inference, and renders an interactive dashboard showing exact token-level feature importance scores (e.g., visually highlighting words such as "suspended", "verify immediately", or "unauthorized access" that contributed positively to the malicious classification score). This interpretability demystifies the AI’s decision-making process, transforming a binary classification output into an actionable educational tool.

The primary limitation of Al-Subaiey et al.'s work lies in its architectural scalability and evaluation scope. The authors primarily focus on desktop email formats with static HTML parsing, omitting API rate-limiting, distributed task queue configurations (such as Celery or RabbitMQ), and containerized microservice orchestration necessary to handle enterprise workloads or high-concurrency web scanning. For our diploma project, Al-Subaiey et al.'s contribution serves as a direct architectural blueprint: our web platform adopts the principle of combining high-accuracy classification with interactive visual explainability, while advancing the engineering stack through a modular React/TypeScript frontend and FastAPI/DevOps backend.

#### Source 11: Liu, R., Lin, Y., Yang, X., Ng, S. H., Divakaran, D. M., & Dong, J. S. (2022). Inferring Phishing Intention via Webpage Appearance and Dynamics: A Deep Vision Based Approach. In *Proceedings of the 31st USENIX Security Symposium (USENIX Security '22)*, pages 1633–1650.

In this premier cybersecurity publication from USENIX Security, Liu and colleagues tackle the pervasive problem of phishing webpages that evade static text and URL filters through dynamic scripts, images, and brand cloning. The authors argue that conventional filters frequently generate intolerable false-positive rates because they identify brand logos without confirming whether the webpage actually exhibits malicious credential-harvesting behavior. To solve this, the authors introduce *PhishIntention*, a state-of-the-art vision-based phishing detection framework that infers both brand identity and phishing intention.

The architecture of *PhishIntention* operates across multiple deep visual stages. It utilizes an object detection convolutional network (Faster R-CNN) to recognize brand logos and identity elements directly from webpage screenshots. It then activates a dynamic verification module that evaluates whether the page contains interactive credential input forms (username/password fields) that violate legitimate brand domain mappings. Tested on massive, live web crawling datasets, *PhishIntention* achieved an unprecedentedly low false-positive rate while detecting evasive phishing sites that completely lacked textual HTML clues.

Despite its exceptional detection efficacy, *PhishIntention* illustrates a critical engineering trade-off: immense system latency and computational cost. Capturing full-page screenshots, rendering dynamic JavaScript within a headless browser, and executing multi-stage deep vision models requires several seconds per URL and heavy GPU clusters. This makes real-time, interactive message analysis on consumer devices infeasible. For our diploma project, Liu et al.'s work establishes an essential standard for security engineering: while deep rendering is suitable for offline enterprise crawling, an interactive web platform analyzing user-submitted messages must prioritize sub-second NLP text inference, utilizing lightweight API calls and heuristic DOM checks rather than heavyweight computer vision rendering.

#### Source 12: Bass, L., Clements, P., & Kazman, R. (2021). *Software Architecture in Practice* (4th Edition). Addison-Wesley Professional.

In this authoritative, foundational software engineering text, Bass, Clements, and Kazman formalize the architectural tactics and design principles required to construct reliable, scalable, and secure software platforms. The authors establish that a software system's non-functional attributes—performance, availability, security, modifiability, and testability—are governed directly by its architectural structure. They define explicit architectural tactics for cybersecurity, dividing them into three core categories: *detecting attacks* (verifying message integrity, monitoring anomalies, intrusion detection), *resisting attacks* (user authentication, authorization, data encryption, limiting resource exposure), and *reacting and recovering from attacks* (graceful degradation, session termination, maintaining audit trails).

The authors thoroughly analyze modern distributed architectural patterns, contrasting monolithic architectures with microservice and service-oriented designs. They detail architectural patterns for performance, including horizontal scaling, caching strategies (in-memory data stores like Redis), asynchronous event handling, and stateless API design. Bass et al. demonstrate that decoupling computationally intensive processing pipelines from user-facing interface gateways prevents system-wide cascading failures and preserves low latency for client interactions.

Although Bass et al. provide a rigorous engineering methodology, their treatise is conceptual and does not specifically address the operational quirks of machine learning pipelines (MLOps), such as model drift, asynchronous GPU/CPU inference scheduling, or tensor serialization. In our diploma project, Bass et al.'s architectural principles directly dictate our system design: Danial Marat's backend and DevOps implementation adheres strictly to the tactics of stateless RESTful API design, containerized service separation (Docker), secure authentication gateways, and decoupled ML processing queues to guarantee high availability and low latency.

#### Source 13: Dragoni, N., Giallorenzo, S., Lluch Lafuente, A., Mazzara, M., Montesi, F., Mustafin, R., & Safina, L. (2017). Microservices: Yesterday, Today, and Tomorrow. In *Present and Ulterior Software Engineering*, pages 195–216. Springer.

Dragoni and colleagues present a foundational, systematic analysis of the microservices architectural paradigm, charting its evolution from monolithic codebases and Service-Oriented Architectures (SOA). The authors define a microservice architecture as a distributed system composed of small, autonomous, independently deployable services, each executing within its own isolated runtime process and communicating via lightweight, language-agnostic mechanisms (typically HTTP/REST APIs or asynchronous message queues). Each service encapsulates a singular business domain capability, adhering to the Single Responsibility Principle.

The authors critically evaluate the trade-offs of microservice architectures. The primary advantages include fine-grained horizontal elasticity (allocating compute resources strictly to bottleneck services), technology heterogeneity (allowing different components to be developed in Python, TypeScript, or Go), continuous integration and deployment (CI/CD) velocity, and robust fault isolation. Conversely, the authors detail the accompanying engineering complexities: distributed data management, network latency across service hops, API contract versioning, and the critical necessity for robust container orchestration and monitoring.

For our diploma project, Dragoni et al.'s architectural taxonomy justifies the structural division of labor and system topology. Because our platform requires heavy mathematical libraries (PyTorch/Scikit-Learn/Python) for machine learning alongside an interactive, reactive user interface (React/TypeScript), a monolithic architecture would result in tight coupling, dependency conflicts, and scaling inefficiencies. By adopting a microservices-aligned architecture, the frontend client, backend API orchestrator, and ML inference core can be built, tested, and containerized independently, directly reflecting the respective contributions of the project team members.

### 2.6. Social Engineering Attack Taxonomy and Human Deception Mechanics

#### Source 14: Heartfield, R., & Loukas, G. (2016). A Taxonomy of Attacks and a Survey of Defence Mechanisms for Semantic Social Engineering Attacks. *ACM Computing Surveys (CSUR)*, 48(3), Article 37, pages 1–39.

In this highly cited, seminal ACM survey, Heartfield and Loukas establish a rigorous theoretical taxonomy of *semantic social engineering attacks*. The authors draw a fundamental architectural distinction between *syntactic attacks* (which exploit vulnerabilities in hardware, operating systems, or software protocols, such as buffer overflows) and *semantic attacks* (which manipulate the meaning, context, and human perception of information to induce users into performing damaging actions). The authors demonstrate that semantic attacks bypass perimeter defenses because the technological communication protocol functions exactly as designed; the exploit occurs entirely within the human cognitive decision loop.

Heartfield and Loukas decompose semantic attacks along multiple analytical dimensions: attack vectors (email, SMS, social media, VoIP), pretexting strategies, targeted cognitive vulnerabilities, and deception mechanisms. They categorize cognitive exploitation techniques based on Robert Cialdini's principles of persuasion: authority (impersonating law enforcement, IT administrators, or executive leadership), scarcity/urgency (false deadlines, impending account termination), social proof (fabricated popularity or peer activity), and reciprocity/greed (bogus rewards or lottery winnings). Furthermore, the authors survey technical and non-technical defense mechanisms, concluding that purely automated blacklists fail against zero-day semantic attacks, while purely educational training suffers from rapid user knowledge decay.

A limitation of Heartfield and Loukas's 2016 survey is its historical timing: it predates the emergence of generative AI and Large Language Models (LLMs), which allow modern threat actors to generate grammatically flawless, hyper-personalized spear-phishing messages at industrial scale. Nevertheless, the authors' taxonomy of cognitive persuasion principles remains deeply relevant. In our diploma project, Heartfield and Loukas's semantic taxonomy directly informs our NLP feature engineering and Explainable AI modules: the web platform is engineered to identify not merely generic spam words, but the explicit psychological levers (urgency, coercive authority, false financial incentives) operating within fraudulent text.

#### Source 15: Hadnagy, C. (2018). *Social Engineering: The Science of Human Hacking* (2nd Edition). John Wiley & Sons.

Christopher Hadnagy provides an authoritative, in-depth practitioner analysis of the psychological frameworks, influence mechanisms, and exploitation vectors driving real-world social engineering. Hadnagy deconstructs the human cognitive vulnerabilities that render individuals susceptible to manipulative communications regardless of their technical literacy. Central to Hadnagy’s analysis is the psychological concept of *framing* and *elicitation*: how attackers structure pretext scenarios that trigger immediate emotional reactions (panic, fear of missing out, heightened curiosity, or desire to comply with authority) while suppressing the rational, deliberative analytical faculties of the human prefrontal cortex (System 2 thinking).

The author examines the practical anatomy of modern communication-based exploits, including vishing (voice phishing) and smishing. He demonstrates that mobile messaging formats (SMS and Telegram) possess psychological trust advantages over email: users typically read text messages within minutes of receipt on intimate personal devices, where display screens compress metadata, truncate URLs, and obscure sender credentials. Hadnagy details how attackers exploit these structural constraints by embedding shortened URLs, utilizing urgency phrases like "Account locked within 24 hours", and impersonating trusted domestic entities (postal delivery services, telecommunication providers, state tax bodies).

While Hadnagy’s work offers profound psychological insights into attacker methodologies, it is written from a descriptive security practitioner viewpoint and lacks algorithmic formulations, formal data models, or automated machine learning detection frameworks. For our diploma project, Hadnagy’s insights provide the foundational domain context required to bridge machine learning detection with active fraud *prevention*. By understanding how pretexts manipulate human emotion, our platform's frontend can present targeted warning banners that explain the psychological manipulation technique detected in the message, empowering users to recognize and resist future deception attempts.

### 2.7. Recent Work on SMS and Smishing Detection (2023–2025)

#### Source 16: Lee, H., Jeong, S., Cho, S., & Choi, E. (2023). Visualization Technology and Deep-Learning for Multilingual Spam Message Detection. *Electronics*, 12(3), 582.

Lee and colleagues point out that most spam research uses English data and that text-based models need separate preprocessing for each language. They convert each message into an image built from the Unicode values of its characters, so that one 2D CNN can process any language without a language-specific tokenizer. On English, Korean and mixed English–Korean messages the 2D CNN reached an average accuracy of about 0.996, slightly above RNN, LSTM and 1D CNN baselines (about 0.987–0.991).

The limitation for our project is that the image representation gives no word-level explanation, so it is hard to tell the user *why* a message was flagged. The study is still important for our planned Russian and Kazakh support (FR-9): it shows that mixed-language messages can be handled by one model, and it gives us a language-independent alternative to compare with per-language TF-IDF vocabularies.

#### Source 17: Mahmud, T., Prince, M. A. H., Ali, M. H., Hossain, M. S., & Andersson, K. (2024). Enhancing Cybersecurity: Hybrid Deep Learning Approaches to Smishing Attack Detection. *Systems*, 12(11), 490.

Mahmud et al. argue that rule-based, heuristic and blacklist approaches cannot keep up with changing smishing tactics. They propose CNN-Bi-GRU, a hybrid of convolutional and bidirectional GRU layers on Word2Vec embeddings, trained on the SMS Phishing Collection dataset, and report 99.82% accuracy, better than the compared approaches.

As with most recent work, the result is obtained on one public dataset and the model is not deployed or explained to users. For our project the paper is mainly a reference point: if our classical baseline reaches a much lower score on the same type of data, a recurrent model is a reasonable next experiment.

#### Source 18: Jain, A. K., Kaur, K., Gupta, N. K., & Khare, A. (2025). Detecting Smishing Messages Using BERT and Advanced NLP Techniques. *SN Computer Science*, 6(2), 109.

Jain et al. classify SMS into three classes — smishing, spam and ham — which is close to our safe / suspicious / fraudulent scheme. They compare TF-IDF features with BERT embeddings and split the three-class task into two binary tasks. BERT embeddings captured context best, while a logistic regression classifier reached the highest reported accuracy of 94% on a dataset with a 4:1 class ratio.

The paper supports two of our design decisions: building a multi-class result from binary decisions, and keeping TF-IDF with logistic regression as a strong and cheap baseline. Its limitation is that it does not cover explanation or deployment.

#### Source 19: Uddin, M. A., Islam, M. N., Maglaras, L., Janicke, H., & Sarker, I. H. (2025). ExplainableDetector: Exploring Transformer-Based Language Modeling Approach for SMS Spam Detection with Explainability Analysis. *Digital Communications and Networks*, 11(5), 1504–1518.

Uddin et al. fine-tune transformer language models on a benchmark SMS spam dataset, use text augmentation to handle class imbalance and compare the results with classical ML baselines. The fine-tuned RoBERTa reached 99.84% accuracy. Explainability methods assign positive and negative contribution scores to words, showing why a message was classified as spam.

This is the closest work to our explanation requirement (FR-3): it shows that word-level contributions are understandable to people. Our baseline gets similar word contributions directly from the coefficients of a linear model on TF-IDF features, without the cost of running a transformer, while a transformer remains an optional comparison.

## 4. Synthesis of Research Gaps and Project Justification

A critical synthesis of the reviewed literature reveals four profound gaps that current academic research and commercial security tools have failed to bridge adequately. The proposed diploma project, **"Intelligent Web Platform for Analysis, Detection, and Prevention of Fraudulent Messages Using Machine Learning Methods,"** is explicitly architected to overcome these limitations.

### Gap 1: The Disconnect Between Standalone Machine Learning Models and Deployable Web Platforms
The overwhelming majority of published research (e.g., Alhogail & Alsabih, 2021; Ghourabi et al., 2020; Sahingoz et al., 2019) focuses entirely on isolated model development, algorithmic parameter tuning, and maximizing offline accuracy metrics in Jupyter notebooks. These studies treat machine learning classification as an end in itself, largely ignoring the severe software engineering challenges required to operationalize models into responsive, reliable, and accessible systems. Real-world users cannot interact with raw Python scripts or serialized pickle files. Conversely, commercial security gateways are frequently closed, proprietary appliances integrated into enterprise email servers, offering no lightweight, cross-platform portal where regular citizens or analysts can verify a suspicious SMS or Telegram message.
*Project Resolution:* The diploma project bridges this chasm by delivering an end-to-end, production-ready web platform. As detailed in the project justification, Danial Marat engineers a scalable, containerized backend utilizing FastAPI, RESTful API conventions, and secure DevOps pipelines, seamlessly integrating with Nurbakyt Abdikasym's responsive React and TypeScript frontend, creating an intuitive public-facing security analysis tool.

### Gap 2: The Black-Box Classification Problem and the Absence of Active User Prevention
Existing security systems are almost universally reactive and opaque. Classifiers output a binary probability score (e.g., "96% Spam" or "Malicious"), providing zero transparency regarding *why* the message was flagged. As demonstrated by Heartfield & Loukas (2016) and Hadnagy (2018), social engineering exploits psychological vulnerabilities (urgency, fear, simulated authority). Pure binary alerts fail to foster user comprehension; users frequently disregard generic warnings because they do not understand the underlying threat. While Al-Subaiey et al. (2024) introduced XAI to email detection, practical explainability mechanisms remain virtually absent from web-based multi-channel message scanners.  
*Project Resolution:* Our platform directly addresses this limitation by embedding Explainable AI (XAI) and proactive prevention into the core user experience. The inference engine highlights specific deceptive tokens (e.g., coercive deadlines, suspicious financial requests) and pairs the classification score with contextual educational warnings. By identifying the exact persuasion tactic in real time, the platform actively trains the user's cognitive faculties, converting a passive detection alert into an active prevention mechanism that inoculates users against future social engineering attempts.

### Gap 3: Dataset Obsolescence and Vulnerability to Adversarial Text Evasion
A pervasive weakness across the literature is the continued reliance on antiquated, historical benchmarks. Foundational datasets like the Almeida et al. (2011) SMS Spam Collection or early Enron corpora reflect spam patterns from over a decade ago (e.g., simple commercial promotions, rudimentary Nigerian 419 advance-fee letters). Contemporary cybercriminals employ sophisticated multi-stage evasion tactics—including adversarial typographical mutations, homoglyphic Cyrillic/Latin character substitution, leetspeak, zero-width spaces, and benign cloud service redirects (Seo et al., 2024; Mishra & Soni, 2022). Models trained exclusively on legacy data exhibit severe performance degradation when exposed to modern zero-day lures.  
*Project Resolution:* The diploma project incorporates robust preprocessing pipelines informed by Seo et al. (2024) that normalize homoglyphs, strip adversarial whitespace, and standardize character substitutions prior to tokenization. Furthermore, the ML training strategy supplements classical benchmarks with contemporary 2022–2026 smishing and messenger fraud corpora, ensuring high generalization against modern fraud vectors.

### Gap 4: Architectural Latency Trade-Offs Between Deep Representations and Real-Time User Experience
Advanced deep learning frameworks—such as heavy Graph Convolutional Networks (Alhogail & Alsabih, 2021) or complex visual rendering engines (Liu et al., 2022)—achieve high detection precision but impose severe computational latency (ranging from several seconds to minutes per scan). In an interactive web environment, users demand instantaneous responses. If a security scanning platform takes more than two seconds to analyze a short message, user abandonment surges, rendering the tool practically useless.  
*Project Resolution:* Our platform solves this latency dilemma through an optimized, multi-stage hybrid architecture governed by the principles of Bass et al. (2021) and Dragoni et al. (2017). Incoming messages are first routed through ultra-fast lexical heuristic screening and domain reputation caches (Redis). For deep textual semantic analysis, rather than utilizing heavyweight 110M-parameter models, we fine-tune lightweight, distilled transformer architectures (such as DistilBERT) optimized for sub-second CPU/GPU inference. This architecture achieves top-tier semantic detection accuracy while guaranteeing the low response latencies required for an exceptional user experience.

## 5. Conclusion

This literature review has critically examined nineteen scientific and foundational publications addressing the technological, mathematical, and architectural dimensions of automated fraudulent message detection. The review substantiates that while advanced deep learning and natural language processing models (particularly hybrid sequence models and transformer encoders) provide superior detection accuracy compared to legacy heuristic rules, their practical utility depends entirely on overcoming systemic challenges in latency, adversarial robustness, interpretability, and web software engineering.

By systematically synthesizing these insights, the diploma project—undertaken by Danial Marat and Nurbakyt Abdikasym under the supervision of Konstantin Latuta—is positioned to make a meaningful engineering contribution. The proposed intelligent web platform harmonizes robust machine learning classification with modern cloud architecture, Explainable AI, and interactive user prevention mechanics, delivering a comprehensive, scalable defense against the escalating threat of digital communication fraud.
